From Machine Instructions to Vibe Coding: The Evolution of Computational Abstraction

Created on November 26, 2026 by Yusuf Brima

2026   ·   Computer Science   Programming Languages   Artificial Intelligence   Computational Abstraction

How humans have progressively moved from communicating directly with computer hardware to expressing computational intentions through increasingly abstract interfaces.

Introduction

Recently, I have been thinking about the growing popularity of what people now call vibe coding. The term was popularized by Andrej Karpathy, an artificial intelligence researcher and former director of AI at Tesla, in a post on X (formerly Twitter) on 2 February 2025. He used it to describe a new way of building software in which a developer interacts with a large language model (LLM), describes what they want, accepts generated code, and iteratively refines the resulting application, sometimes without even examining the underlying code.

There is considerable excitement around this idea. We increasingly see demonstrations of people building websites, mobile applications, data analysis tools, and even relatively complex software systems by simply describing what they want in natural language. Some have interpreted this as a fundamental transformation in software development, while others have gone as far as suggesting that conventional programming skills may eventually become unnecessary.

But I have started wondering whether we are actually witnessing something fundamentally new, or whether we are simply giving a new name to an idea that has existed in computer science for several decades.

To understand why I think this way, it is worth taking a step back and looking at the history of computation, particularly the evolution of programming languages and the different levels of abstraction through which humans have interacted with computers.

I find this history particularly interesting because, when viewed from a broader perspective, there seems to be a recurring pattern: we continuously develop new ways of expressing computational intentions while progressively moving away from the underlying machinery responsible for executing them.

Perhaps understanding this historical progression can help us put the current excitement around vibe coding into a much broader perspective.

From Human Instructions to Machine Instructions

Before discussing programming languages, I think it is important to understand what programming actually means.

At its most fundamental level, programming is the process of specifying a sequence of instructions that a computational system can execute to accomplish a particular task.

A computer does not inherently understand what we mean when we say that we want to calculate an average, sort a collection of numbers, or identify patterns in a dataset. These intentions have to be translated into representations and operations that the underlying computational machinery can execute.

This translation has historically been one of the central challenges in computer science.

Consider the work of Charles Babbage and Ada Lovelace in the nineteenth century. Babbage conceptualized the Analytical Engine, a mechanical general-purpose computing machine, while Lovelace recognized that such a machine could potentially manipulate symbols beyond numerical quantities. In her 1843 notes on the Analytical Engine, she described an algorithm for calculating Bernoulli numbers, an achievement widely regarded as an important milestone in the history of computer programming.

Of course, the Analytical Engine was never completed during Babbage’s lifetime. Nevertheless, the ideas surrounding programmable mechanical computation anticipated many of the principles that would later become central to modern computing.

Fast-forward to the twentieth century, when electronic computers began to emerge.

Early computers were programmed using very low-level representations of instructions. In some cases, programming involved physically configuring switches, connecting cables, or using punched cards. As electronic computers developed, instructions became increasingly represented as binary sequences that could be executed directly by the processor.

This brings us to what is commonly called first-generation programming languages (1GL), or machine language.

Machine language is the lowest-level representation of instructions that a processor can execute directly. It consists of binary patterns representing operations, memory addresses, and other information required by the processor.

For example, an instruction might tell the processor to move data from one location to another, perform an arithmetic operation, or compare two values.

The important thing to understand is that programmers working at this level had very little abstraction between themselves and the underlying hardware.

If they wanted a computer to perform a particular operation, they had to express that operation using the specific instruction representations understood by the processor.

This was obviously cumbersome, difficult to maintain, and highly dependent on the architecture of the machine being programmed.

But then something remarkable happened.

The Introduction of Symbolic Instructions

As computer systems became increasingly sophisticated, there was a need to make programming less dependent on manually interpreting binary instructions.

This led to the development of assembly languages, commonly classified as second-generation programming languages (2GL).

Assembly language allows programmers to represent machine instructions using symbolic names, known as mnemonics, rather than writing their binary equivalents directly.

For example, instead of working with a sequence of binary digits, a programmer might write:

MOV AX, 5
MOV BX, 3
ADD AX, BX

Here, MOV represents a data movement operation, while ADD represents an arithmetic addition operation.

These instructions are much easier for humans to read and reason about than their binary equivalents.

However, the computer does not directly understand these symbolic representations. They must first be translated into machine instructions.

This is where an assembler comes in.

An assembler is a program that translates assembly language instructions into machine code that a processor can execute.

One important historical figure in this transition was Kathleen Booth, a British mathematician and computer scientist who developed one of the earliest assembly languages while working on the ARC computer at Birkbeck College in London in the late 1940s.

This was an important development because it introduced another layer of abstraction between human reasoning and the underlying hardware.

We no longer needed to remember binary instruction codes. We could use symbolic representations that were easier to understand and manipulate.

However, programming was still relatively close to the architecture of the machine. We were essentially expressing operations that corresponded directly to what the processor needed to execute.

But we did not stop there.

The Emergence of High-Level Programming Languages

The next major development was the emergence of high-level programming languages, generally associated with third-generation programming languages (3GL).

A high-level programming language is a language that allows programmers to express computational operations using abstractions that are considerably further removed from the underlying hardware.

Unlike assembly language, a high-level language does not require programmers to explicitly specify individual processor instructions or manage every low-level operation.

Consider a simple example in Python:

a = 5
b = 3
result = a + b
print(result)

This is considerably easier to understand than the equivalent sequence of machine instructions.

We can express the computational intention without worrying about how the processor implements the addition operation.

But how does the computer execute this code?

This brings us to another important concept: the compiler.

A compiler is a software program that translates source code written in a programming language into another representation, often machine code or an intermediate representation that can subsequently be executed.

An interpreter, on the other hand, is a program that executes instructions from source code or an intermediate representation, typically without requiring the entire program to be translated into a standalone executable beforehand. The distinction is not always absolute because modern language implementations often combine compilation and interpretation.

The important point is that the programmer can now work with increasingly expressive representations while other software components take responsibility for translating those representations into instructions that the machine can execute.

There were several important figures behind this development.

One particularly influential figure was Grace Hopper, an American computer scientist and United States Navy officer. Hopper worked on early computing systems and developed the A-0 system, which is often described as one of the earliest compiler-like systems. Her work helped establish the idea that programmers could write instructions using symbolic representations that would subsequently be translated into machine-executable operations.

Another important figure was John Backus, who led the IBM team responsible for developing FORTRAN (Formula Translation), introduced in 1957.

FORTRAN was particularly important because it demonstrated that scientific and mathematical computations could be expressed in a high-level language without necessarily sacrificing the execution efficiency of manually written machine-oriented code.

This was a significant achievement.

At the time, some programmers were sceptical that a compiler could generate machine code efficient enough to compete with hand-written assembly language. FORTRAN helped demonstrate that this was possible.

And so, we entered an era in which programmers could express increasingly complex computational operations without having to concern themselves with the individual instructions required by the underlying processor.

Languages such as FORTRAN, COBOL, C, C++, and later Java and Python became important parts of this development.

Think about how much abstraction has already happened here.

A programmer writing Python does not need to understand how the processor implements an addition operation, how memory registers are manipulated, or how individual instructions are encoded in binary. These details are handled by lower layers of the computational system.

We had effectively moved the responsibility for translating human-readable instructions into machine-executable operations away from the programmer.

And this brings me to another interesting development.

The Pursuit of Declarative Programming

As programming languages evolved, another important question emerged: do programmers always need to specify how a computation should be performed, or can they simply describe what they want the computer to accomplish?

This distinction is central to understanding the development of declarative programming.

Procedural programming is a programming approach in which the programmer specifies the sequence of operations required to accomplish a particular task.

Declarative programming, on the other hand, is an approach in which the programmer describes the desired result or the relationships that should hold, leaving the execution system to determine how to produce that result.

To understand the difference, consider a simple example involving databases.

Imagine that we have a database containing information about thousands of patients, and we want to retrieve all patients whose age is greater than 50.

We could theoretically write a program that iterates through every record, examines the age of each patient, and adds qualifying records to a new collection.

But what if we could simply describe the information we wanted?

This is precisely what Structured Query Language (SQL) allows us to do.

SQL (Structured Query Language) is a declarative programming language used primarily to define, manipulate, and retrieve information from relational databases. A relational database organizes information into tables consisting of rows and columns, with relationships between tables established through shared attributes.

For example:

SELECT *
FROM patients
WHERE age > 50;

This instruction tells the database management system to retrieve all records from the patients table where the patient’s age exceeds 50.

Notice something interesting here.

We have not specified how the database should search through the records, how it should organize its memory, or which execution strategy it should use.

We have simply described the information we want.

The database management system determines how to execute the query, potentially using indexes, query optimization, and other computational techniques.

This is another significant abstraction.

And there are interesting historical figures behind this development.

In 1970, Edgar F. Codd, a British computer scientist working at IBM, published an influential paper introducing the relational model of data. His work provided the mathematical foundations for organizing and querying information using relations, which eventually became central to modern relational database systems.

Building on Codd’s work, Donald Chamberlin and Raymond Boyce developed SEQUEL (Structured English Query Language) at IBM in the early 1970s. The language was subsequently renamed SQL and became one of the most widely used ways of interacting with databases.

Their motivation was particularly interesting: they wanted to develop a language through which people could interact with relational databases without necessarily requiring extensive mathematical or programming expertise.

When I think about this development, I find it difficult not to notice the recurring pattern.

We are progressively moving away from specifying individual computational operations and towards expressing higher-level intentions.

The programmer is increasingly concerned with describing the problem rather than explicitly implementing every procedure required to solve it.

And this brings us to another interesting development in the history of programming languages.

Fourth- and Fifth-Generation Programming Languages

The distinction between procedural and declarative programming also provides some context for understanding what are commonly referred to as fourth- and fifth-generation programming languages.

The classification of programming languages into generations is not universally standardized, and there is some overlap between the categories. Nevertheless, it provides a useful conceptual framework for understanding the evolution of programming abstractions.

Fourth-generation programming languages (4GL) generally refer to languages that provide higher-level, often domain-specific ways of expressing computational operations. Many emphasize describing what should be accomplished rather than specifying every individual computational step.

SQL is a familiar example. Other examples include SAS and MATLAB, although their programming paradigms and capabilities differ considerably.

The important point is that these languages allow users to express computational operations using abstractions tailored to particular tasks or domains.

But then there was another ambition.

What if we could move beyond specifying computational operations altogether and instead describe problems using facts, relationships, logical rules, and constraints?

This was one of the central ideas associated with fifth-generation programming languages (5GL).

An important example is Prolog, a logic programming language developed through the work of Alain Colmerauer, Philippe Roussel, and Robert Kowalski in the early 1970s.

Logic programming is a programming paradigm in which computational problems are expressed using logical statements, facts, and rules. Instead of explicitly specifying every step of an algorithm, the programmer describes relationships and conditions that a system can use to derive answers.

Consider the following Prolog example:

parent(john, mary).
parent(mary, peter).
grandparent(X, Z) :-
parent(X, Y),
parent(Y, Z).

Here, we define two facts: John is the parent of Mary, and Mary is the parent of Peter.

We then define a rule stating that someone is a grandparent if they are the parent of another person who is themselves a parent.

If we ask Prolog whether John is Peter’s grandparent, the system can use the facts and rules to infer the answer.

Notice that we have not explicitly implemented a procedure for traversing a family tree. We have described relationships and a logical rule from which the desired conclusion can be derived.

Interestingly, these ideas were also connected to broader ambitions in artificial intelligence.

In 1982, Japan launched the Fifth Generation Computer Systems project, an ambitious national research initiative aimed at developing computers oriented towards knowledge-based information processing.

The project explored logic programming, parallel inference, and new computational architectures intended to support intelligent systems.

The Institute for New Generation Computer Technology (ICOT) was established to coordinate the work, which continued until 1992.

I find this historical development particularly interesting because it demonstrates that the ambition to make computers understand higher-level human intentions is not something that emerged with large language models.

Computer scientists were exploring ways of expressing computational problems in increasingly abstract forms long before generative AI became possible.

The underlying ambition was to reduce the amount of explicit programming required from humans while allowing computational systems to take greater responsibility for determining how problems should be solved.

And then something remarkable happened.

And Then Came Generative AI

Now, fast-forward to the present.

We have large language models capable of interpreting natural-language instructions, generating source code, interpreting errors, modifying existing implementations, and interacting with development environments.

A large language model (LLM) is an artificial intelligence model trained on large quantities of text and other data to learn patterns in language and generate contextually relevant responses. These models can also learn patterns in source code, allowing them to generate, explain, and modify computer programs.

We can describe an application in ordinary English and ask an AI system to build it.

We can ask it to implement a machine learning pipeline, develop a web application, create a database schema, or debug an existing codebase.

And increasingly, these systems can perform several of these activities autonomously.

This is what has popularized the term vibe coding.

On 2 February 2025, Andrej Karpathy introduced the term in a post on X, describing a way of building software in which he interacted with an LLM through natural-language instructions, accepted generated changes, and iteratively refined the application without necessarily examining the underlying code.

It is worth emphasizing that Karpathy did not invent AI-assisted programming, natural-language programming, or automated code generation. These ideas have much longer histories. His contribution was to give an emerging style of interaction a memorable name that subsequently gained considerable popularity.

But when I look at this development through the historical progression described earlier, I find it difficult to see it as an entirely new paradigm.

We have been pursuing the idea of reducing the distance between human computational intentions and machine execution for a very long time.

The difference is that we now have systems capable of interpreting relatively unconstrained natural-language instructions and generating executable implementations from them.

There is, however, an important distinction between what we had previously and what we have today.

Traditional programming languages, including declarative languages, operate within formally defined syntactic and semantic rules.

Syntax refers to the structural rules governing how instructions must be written in a programming language. Semantics refers to the meaning of those instructions and what they are supposed to accomplish.

For example, a SQL query must follow the language’s syntax, and its semantics determine which records it is intended to retrieve.

A compiler translates a program according to the rules of the language.

Large language models operate differently. They learn statistical patterns from enormous collections of data and generate outputs based on the context and instructions they receive.

Consequently, when I describe an application in natural language, the AI system is not necessarily translating my instructions through a deterministic sequence of predefined programming rules. It is generating an implementation based on its learned representations of programming concepts, software architectures, and computational patterns.

This introduces a fundamentally different mechanism for bridging the gap between human intentions and executable instructions.

And perhaps this is where the real novelty lies.

The Evolution of Computational Abstraction

At this point, I think it is useful to step back and look at the developments we have discussed as part of a broader historical progression.

What emerges is a recurring pattern in which humans progressively delegate lower-level computational responsibilities to increasingly sophisticated systems.

Of course, this should not be interpreted as a strict chronological replacement of one programming paradigm by another. Machine language, assembly language, high-level programming languages, and declarative languages all continue to coexist. Each serves different computational purposes.

Nevertheless, the broader direction is interesting.

We moved from writing machine instructions to writing symbolic instructions. We moved from symbolic instructions to high-level programming languages. We developed declarative languages that allowed us to specify desired outcomes without explicitly describing every computational procedure.

Now, we increasingly have artificial systems that can interpret natural-language descriptions and construct the necessary computational artefacts on our behalf.

Perhaps this is simply another step in the same historical trajectory.

But there is another question that I find particularly interesting.

Are Programming Languages Becoming Less Important?

Are we witnessing the evolution of programming languages, or are we witnessing the evolution of the interfaces through which humans communicate computational intentions?

I think these are two different things.

A natural-language interface does not necessarily constitute a new programming language. We can interact with an AI system using ordinary English without English itself becoming a formally defined programming language.

The AI system effectively acts as an intermediary between human intentions and the formal computational representations required by the underlying machine.

In this sense, perhaps what is evolving is not necessarily the programming language itself, but the mechanism through which computational artefacts are specified, constructed, and modified.

Think about the different levels of abstraction we have developed over the years.

We no longer need to manually translate every computational operation into machine instructions. We can use high-level languages and allow compilers and interpreters to handle the lower-level details.

We no longer need to explicitly implement every database retrieval operation. We can use declarative queries and allow database management systems to determine how to execute them.

And now, we increasingly have artificial intelligence systems that can translate natural-language descriptions into executable implementations.

Perhaps the more interesting development is that we are beginning to separate the act of specifying a computational problem from the act of implementing its solution.

The human increasingly specifies the intention, while the machine increasingly constructs the implementation.

However, I would not interpret this as the disappearance of programming itself. Rather, I think it represents a shift in where programming effort is concentrated.

The Limits of Abstraction

But I have started wondering whether there is also a limit to how far we can push this abstraction.

Every abstraction hides certain implementation details from the person interacting with the system. This is precisely what makes abstraction useful.

A Python developer does not need to understand assembly language to implement a machine learning algorithm. A database administrator does not need to understand the internal query execution plan to retrieve records using SQL.

However, there are situations where understanding those hidden details becomes important.

When a database query becomes inefficient, we may need to understand query optimization. When a Python program consumes too much memory, we may need to understand memory management. When a machine learning model fails to generalize, we may need to investigate the underlying statistical and computational assumptions.

The same applies to AI-generated software.

A system that generates a working application does not necessarily understand all the underlying assumptions, architectural decisions, security implications, or failure modes associated with that application.

For example, developing a machine learning model is not simply a matter of generating a Python script that imports PyTorch, defines a neural network, and executes a training loop.

We still need to understand the statistical assumptions, the data-generating process, the optimization objective, the evaluation methodology, and whether the resulting model actually learns what we intended it to learn.

The fact that an AI system can generate an implementation does not automatically mean that the implementation is correct.

And perhaps this introduces a new kind of abstraction gap: the distance between what we ask a system to construct and our ability to understand, verify, and maintain what it actually constructs.

I do not think this is necessarily an argument against using these tools. Rather, it is a reminder that abstraction and understanding are not the same thing.

Finding the Balance

I find the historical evolution of programming particularly fascinating because it reflects something much broader about human technological development.

We continuously develop tools that allow us to externalize cognitive effort and operate at increasingly higher levels of abstraction.

We no longer need to manually manipulate electrical signals to perform computations. We do not need to write machine instructions to develop software. We do not even need to explicitly implement every computational procedure when declarative systems can determine how to execute our requests.

And now, we increasingly have artificial systems that can translate relatively unconstrained human intentions into executable computational artefacts.

Perhaps this is not the beginning of an entirely new era of programming. Perhaps it is another manifestation of a much older ambition: making computers increasingly accessible to human reasoning, while progressively abstracting away the complexity of their underlying machinery.

There is certainly something remarkable about being able to describe an idea in ordinary language and watch a computational system construct an implementation of it.

But I think we should be careful not to confuse the novelty of the interface with the novelty of the underlying intellectual ambition.

We have been moving towards this for decades.

Perhaps the more interesting question is not whether vibe coding represents the future of programming, but how far we can push the boundary between human intention and machine execution without losing our ability to understand the computational artefacts we create.

Because, ultimately, the objective of abstraction was never simply to hide complexity. It was to make increasingly complex things manageable.

And perhaps that distinction becomes even more important as we begin delegating the construction of those things to artificial intelligence.
